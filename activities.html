Module 1

Ready to work safely

Applied activity 1.1Contamination-chain photo auditNot started

Purpose: Practise connecting visible preparation choices to invisible transfer pathways.

Materials

  • Section readiness visual
  • Three colours of sticky note or a digital annotation tool
  • A4 person-to-place checklist

Ready-to-use stimulus

Scene A — not ready: A phone has just been used, hair is loose, hands are dry and unwashed, an everyday bag touches the shared bench and the apron is not on.

Scene B — prepared: The phone and bag are stored, hair is restrained, hands are washed and dried, a clean apron is on and the cleared work zone is ready.

Steps

  1. Mark possible biological, physical or allergen sources in the first scene.
  2. Draw how one contaminant could reach ready-to-eat food.
  3. Match each arrow to a control in the prepared scene.
  4. Order the controls by when they should happen.
  5. Peer-check that every control includes a reason.

Evidence to keep: An annotated contamination chain and revised five-point checklist, labelled formative practice.

Applied activity 1.2Bench-route control challengeNot started

Purpose: Practise hazard classification and locate controls at real transfer points.

Materials

  • Bench visual
  • Hazard and control cards
  • Timer-history cards

Steps

  1. Sort hazards as biological, chemical, physical or allergen.
  2. Place each at its likely source.
  3. Draw its route to food or a person.
  4. Place a separation, cleaning, time or temperature control.
  5. Apply one timer card and defend use, refrigerate or discard.

Evidence to keep: An annotated bench map and written defence of one time-temperature decision.

Applied activity 1.3First-action decision carouselNot started

Purpose: Rehearse calm, proportionate incident decisions.

Materials

  • Four scene cards
  • Hazard–risk–control sheet
  • Stop, isolate and tell cards

Ready-to-use stimulus

  • Card A: a drink spill crosses a walking path.
  • Card B: an exposed hot tray is left near the bench edge.
  • Card C: small fragments are found beside ready-to-eat food.
  • Card D: a damaged electrical lead is noticed before equipment is switched on.

For every card, students stop work and act only within their training and the teacher’s directions.

Steps

  1. Record one observed fact and possible harm per scene.
  2. Order stop, isolate and tell actions.
  3. Cross out actions beyond student training.
  4. Add one preventive workflow change.
  5. Peer-check that the change breaks the pathway.

Evidence to keep: Four decision records and one expanded before-and-after redesign.

Module 2

Food and the body

Applied activity 2.1More than one nutrientNot started

Purpose: Break the one-food–one-nutrient misconception.

Materials

  • Food-image cards
  • Nutrient labels
  • Three-function sorting mat

Steps

  1. Choose six foods from at least four food groups.
  2. Attach at least two defensible nutrient labels to each.
  3. Place foods across one or more function zones.
  4. Find two foods with one overlapping function but different profiles.
  5. Explain why removing an entire group reduces variety.

Evidence to keep: A photographed sorting mat and 100-word explanation of one complementary pairing.

Applied activity 2.2Same energy, different contributionNot started

Purpose: Combine label evidence with meal context.

Materials

  • Two de-identified panels
  • Context cards
  • Comparison frame

Ready-to-use stimulus

Fictional context: Alex eats lunch at 1.00 pm and has an 80-minute sport session at 3.30 pm. A cool pack and drinking water are available. Allergies, cultural needs and medical requirements are not supplied.

  • Option A: wholegrain chicken-and-salad wrap, plain yoghurt, banana and water.
  • Option B: cheese-and-cracker snack pack, apple purée pouch, 300 mL flavoured milk and water.

Decide which better fits this context, then name the portion or label evidence still needed. Do not judge from energy alone.

Steps

  1. Check whether stated serves match.
  2. Compare per 100 g, then planned serve.
  3. List useful contributions from each option.
  4. Draw a context and select the stronger fit.
  5. Draw a new context and reconsider.

Evidence to keep: A comparison frame with two context-dependent decisions.

Module 3

Reading food evidence

Applied activity 3.1Three-pass label scanNot started

Purpose: Build an efficient routine for safety, comparison and portion evidence.

Materials

  • Two fictional practice labels supplied below
  • Three-pass sheet
  • Highlighters

Ready-to-use stimulus

All values are fictional practice data—not real products.

Plain yoghurt A
Serve 170 g; one serve per pack. Per serve / per 100 g: energy 510 / 300 kJ; protein 11.9 / 7.0 g; total fat 4.3 / 2.5 g; saturated fat 2.7 / 1.6 g; carbohydrate 8.5 / 5.0 g; sugars 8.5 / 5.0 g; sodium 85 / 50 mg. Ingredients: milk, milk solids, cream, live cultures. Contains milk. Use by 10 Sep 20XX. Keep refrigerated at or below 5 °C. Claim: no added sugar; contains naturally occurring milk sugars.

Vanilla yoghurt B
Serve 125 g; one serve per pack. Per serve / per 100 g: energy 500 / 400 kJ; protein 5.6 / 4.5 g; total fat 3.0 / 2.4 g; saturated fat 1.9 / 1.5 g; carbohydrate 17.5 / 14.0 g; sugars 16.3 / 13.0 g; sodium 75 / 60 mg. Ingredients: milk, sugar, milk solids, cream, natural vanilla flavour, live cultures. Contains milk. Use by 10 Sep 20XX. Keep refrigerated at or below 5 °C.

Steps

  1. Highlight allergens, date and storage.
  2. Circle serve size and compare per 100 g.
  3. Underline the first three ingredients and sugar terms.
  4. Write one supported conclusion per pass.
  5. Write one tempting but unsupported conclusion.

Evidence to keep: Two annotated labels and an evidence-versus-inference record.

Applied activity 3.2Comparison refereeNot started

Purpose: Detect and repair unfair label comparisons.

Materials

  • Four flawed claims
  • Two fictional panels
  • Referee checklist

Ready-to-use stimulus

Fair-comparison purpose: Choose one yoghurt component for a portable lunch. Use fictional labels A and B from activity 3.1. Compare per 100 g first, then the whole tub. Cold storage and the milk allergen apply to both.

Steps

  1. Identify the hidden purpose or missing audience.
  2. Check product and amount comparability.
  3. Circle conclusions based on unequal serves or one number.
  4. Rewrite using per 100 g and a realistic portion.
  5. Issue a conditional decision and name one gap.

Evidence to keep: Four corrected claims and one fully worked referee decision.

Applied activity 3.3Thirty-second impression resetNot started

Purpose: Make persuasive framing visible before full evaluation.

Materials

  • Fictional promotion
  • Timer
  • Claim–evidence–omission table
  • Official links

Ready-to-use stimulus

Fictional practice ad — Berry Crunch: “Made with real berries.” “Natural energy.” “Fuel your best.”

Ingredients: wholegrain oats 42%, yoghurt-style coating 26% [sugar, vegetable oil, milk powder], sugar, rice crisps, berry purée 2%, honey, flavour and salt. Contains gluten-containing oats and milk; may contain peanuts and tree nuts.

Evidence check: Berry purée is 2%. “Fuel your best” has no defined performance measure. “Natural” is not explained.

Steps

  1. View for thirty seconds and record three impressions.
  2. Label each as evidence, inference or feeling.
  3. Check two claims against label or official evidence.
  4. Add one omitted health, audience or environment question.
  5. Rewrite the promotion more fairly.

Evidence to keep: Before-and-after impressions, evidence table and revised ethical promotion.

Module 4

Needs, habits and influences

Applied activity 4.1Variety evidence sortNot started

Purpose: Practise matching observable food features to defensible contributions while rejecting exaggerated claims.

Materials

  • Printed or digital food-photo cards
  • Contribution cards: fibre, water, colour variety, food-group variety
  • Claim cards, including two deliberately exaggerated examples

Ready-to-use stimulus

  • “This purple smoothie detoxes your body overnight.”
  • “One spoonful of chia prevents heart disease.”

Repair each fictional claim using cautious evidence language such as contributes to, supports or may.

Steps

  1. Sort food photos by more than one possible contribution.
  2. Match each group to a cautious evidence statement.
  3. Identify and rewrite the two exaggerated claims.
  4. Redesign one familiar snack by adding variety for a named purpose.
  5. Peer-check whether the reason is accurate, realistic and non-moralising.

Evidence to keep: One annotated sort, two corrected claims and a photographed or written snack redesign saved as formative practice.

Applied activity 4.2Lifecycle meal adaptation studioNot started

Purpose: Practise adapting one base food idea for different stated contexts without assuming ability, taste or health status.

Materials

  • Four fictional, privacy-safe user cards
  • One adaptable base-meal card
  • Design-chain worksheet or digital template
  • Australian Guide to Healthy Eating reference

Ready-to-use stimulus

Base idea: bean, roast-vegetable and cheese wrap with water.

  • Case A: Jordan, 14, has ten minutes for lunch, carries an insulated bag and has sport after school. They prefer familiar savoury foods. Budget, allergies and cultural needs are not supplied.
  • Case B: Ari, 72, eats lunch at home, prefers softer textures and an easy-to-hold serve, and enjoys eating with family. Medical, allergy and cultural requirements are not supplied.

Use stated facts only. Circle every unknown rather than filling it with a stereotype.

Steps

  1. Underline facts in each user card and circle unknowns.
  2. Choose two users and identify a distinct design priority for each.
  3. Adapt the base meal using ingredients, texture, portion, portability or preparation features.
  4. Annotate the reason for each change and one practical check.
  5. Swap designs and flag any stereotype or unsupported prescription.

Evidence to keep: Two annotated adaptations plus a peer-review note showing one confirmed need and one unresolved assumption.

Applied activity 5.1Pattern claim clinicNot started

Purpose: Practise turning government data into accurate claims, limits and testable design opportunities.

Materials

  • Current ABS excerpt supplied below
  • Claim cards
  • Scope-and-limit checklist
  • Design-response canvas

Ready-to-use stimulus

ABS data card — Australian children aged 2–17, 2023: 2.1% met the vegetables, legumes and beans recommendation; 50.7% met the fruit recommendation.

Limit: These are national estimates for a broad age group. They do not describe this school, explain causes or judge an individual.

Source: Australian Bureau of Statistics, Consumption of food groups from the Australian Dietary Guidelines, released 10 July 2026.

Steps

  1. Mark source, population, date, measure and unit.
  2. Sort claim cards into supported, overgeneralised or unsupported.
  3. Repair two overgeneralised claims using cautious wording.
  4. Identify one barrier the data suggests and one it does not prove.
  5. Draft a small design response and a success measure.

Evidence to keep: A checked data excerpt, two repaired claims and one proportionate response canvas saved as formative practice.

Applied activity 5.2Convenience system mapNot started

Purpose: Make the technologies, decisions and control points behind one convenience food visible.

Materials

  • Convenience-food case card
  • Stage cards from production to eating
  • Benefit, risk and trade-off tokens
  • FSANZ or NSW Food Authority reference extract

Ready-to-use stimulus

Fictional context: Casey wants a vegetable-and-chickpea rice bowl at home within 30 minutes. A microwave and fridge are available. Budget, allergies and preferences still need confirmation.

  • Home-assembled: microwave brown rice, frozen mixed vegetables, canned chickpeas and yoghurt-herb sauce; about 12 minutes; ingredient and storage information available; one can and two food packs.
  • Delivered: a similar bowl from a nearby café; estimated 25–40 minutes; single-use bowl and bag; ingredients, allergen controls, portion and arrival temperature not yet confirmed.

Map both through sourcing, preparation, packaging, transport, handover and storage/eating.

Steps

  1. Sequence the stages from production to eating.
  2. Attach a technology and intended benefit to each relevant stage.
  3. Place safety, cost, access and waste tokens where they arise.
  4. Identify one control point that cannot be skipped.
  5. Redesign one stage to improve responsibility while preserving the main convenience benefit.

Evidence to keep: A photographed or digital system map and a two-part redesign justification saved as formative practice.

Applied activity 5.3Design decision matrix sprintNot started

Purpose: Practise comparing alternatives fairly and making an evidence-based selection before practical work.

Materials

  • Privacy-safe user brief
  • Three concept cards
  • Weighted decision matrix
  • Food label and safety evidence pack

Ready-to-use stimulus

Fictional brief: Jamie needs a cold school lunch for 1.00 pm. An insulated bag is available, the ingredient-cost target is no more than $5 and home assembly time is 15 minutes. Allergies, cultural needs and medical requirements are not supplied and must be checked before any practical.

  • Concept A: chickpea-and-vegetable wrap.
  • Concept B: brown-rice, bean and vegetable salad.
  • Concept C: yoghurt, oat and fruit pot with a separate savoury vegetable component.

These are concepts, not approved recipes. Set criteria, compare fairly and name the practical evidence still required.

Steps

  1. Turn the brief into criteria and constraints.
  2. Assign a transparent importance weighting to each criterion.
  3. Score three concepts and record evidence for every score.
  4. Stress-test the leading concept against one changed constraint.
  5. Write a recommendation and one prototype test rather than treating the score as automatic truth.

Evidence to keep: A completed decision matrix, evidence notes and a stress-tested recommendation saved as formative practice.

Module 6

Health, guidance and HelloEats

Applied activity 6.1Pattern–mechanism–support triadsNot started

Purpose: Practise separating observed evidence, a possible nutrition mechanism and a safe response.

Materials

  • Fictional case cards
  • Pattern cards
  • Mechanism cards
  • Support and not-in-my-role cards

Ready-to-use stimulus

All cases are fictional. Describe evidence, use may or could, and do not diagnose or comment on bodies.

  • Case 1: Across ten school days, Morgan’s record shows no breakfast on seven days and crackers only at lunch on four days because household food ran short. Morgan reports afternoon tiredness. No diagnosis or test result is supplied.
  • Case 2: Across fourteen evenings, a household record includes large takeaway serves and sweet drinks on nine nights during late-work weeks. Portion actually eaten, activity, sleep, medicines and health data are unknown.
  • Case 3: Taylor’s two-week record shows regular food occasions but a narrow range dominated by refined-grain snacks and sweet drinks; legumes, wholegrains, fruit and vegetables appear rarely. Total energy needs and medical information are unknown.

Steps

  1. Underline only observable evidence in each case.
  2. Match a possible mechanism using may or could language.
  3. Add context cards that might strengthen or weaken the interpretation.
  4. Select a respectful food-system support and an appropriate help pathway.
  5. Reject any diagnosis, body judgement, guarantee or public disclosure card and explain why.

Evidence to keep: Three completed evidence–mechanism–support triads with a boundary explanation saved as formative practice.

Applied activity 6.2Mechanism and claim clinicNot started

Purpose: Practise building accurate risk explanations and repairing diagnostic, stigmatising or guaranteed-prevention claims.

Materials

  • Condition mechanism cards
  • Risk and protective factor cards
  • Claim cards
  • Professional-boundary prompt

Ready-to-use stimulus

Mechanism starters: use the section 6.2 explanations for anaemia, cardiovascular health, bone health and type 2 diabetes. For one condition, record the normal body-system job, a possible nutrition mechanism, interacting modifiable and non-modifiable factors, a cautious protective action and the limit of a Year 9 conclusion.

Steps

  1. Build one body-system mechanism chain.
  2. Sort factors into modifiable, non-modifiable, environmental or needs-more-context.
  3. Identify three overclaims or stigmatising statements.
  4. Rewrite each using supports, contributes to or can reduce risk.
  5. Add an inclusive design response and a private help pathway.

Evidence to keep: One mechanism chain, a classified factor set and three repaired claims saved as formative practice.

Applied activity 6.3Draft HelloEats evidence sprintNot started

Purpose: Integrate guidance, label comparison and responsible design while keeping formative practice separate from formal task conditions.

Materials

  • Fictional user and occasion card
  • Two similar product labels
  • Australian Guide to Healthy Eating reference
  • Draft concept canvas
  • Current-notification check strip

Ready-to-use stimulus

Use each tool for its own job:

  • AGHE: plan food-group variety across an eating pattern.
  • Nutrition panel: compare energy and selected nutrients per 100 g or 100 mL, then per planned serve.
  • Ingredient list and allergen declaration: identify contents and safety information.
  • Health Star Rating: one aid for comparing similar packaged foods, not a whole-diet verdict.
  • Current teacher-issued notification: the only source for dates, marks, submission and practical conditions.

Reuse the paired fictional yoghurt labels from activities 3.1 and 3.2 as the component-comparison evidence. This site activity remains formative and is not submission.

Steps

  1. Turn the fictional user card into criteria and constraints.
  2. Compare the two labels using per 100 g or mL, serve, ingredients and rating only where appropriate.
  3. Sketch a ready-to-eat concept and annotate food-group, appeal, cost and packaging decisions.
  4. Add safety and allergen confirmation points without inventing a practical recipe.
  5. Complete the status strip: formative evidence, not submitted, and formal conditions come only from the current teacher-issued notification.

Evidence to keep: A draft concept canvas, completed product comparison and explicit status strip saved as formative practice only.
